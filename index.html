Inference for Proportions

Chapter 8 — confidence intervals and significance tests for one proportion, and for the difference between two.

A note before you start, Sezen

This lecture answers one question: what can a sample tell you about a population? You count 812 students out of 1 500 who went to Florida. The 54.1% is a fact about your sample and nobody doubts it. The interesting number is the one you cannot see — the true percentage among all students — and this whole lecture is the machinery for saying something defensible about it.

There are only two moves, and they use the same three ingredients. A confidence interval says "estimate ± margin of error" and hands you a range of plausible values. A significance test assumes a specific value is true and asks how strange your sample would be if it were. Everything else in the lecture — z*, standard errors, pooled versus unpooled, plus-four, sample size — is detail hanging off those two moves.

You will use this constantly at NN. Every engagement score, every offer-acceptance rate, every "72% of managers say…" is a proportion from a sample, and the honest version of that sentence always carries a margin of error. The most valuable habit this lecture can give you is the reflex to ask "out of how many?" — because with n = 40 a difference of eight percentage points is noise, and with n = 4 000 it is a finding.

How to use this platform. Work through the Lessons in order; each is one diagram, five bullets and a check. The calculators are there so you can stop arithmetic being the hard part — change a number and watch which piece of the formula moves. 🧵 Synthesis

The single thesis of this lecture

Every procedure in Chapter 8 is the same sentence with different nouns: your estimate, plus or minus so many standard errors. The standard error is always the spread of the sampling distribution — how much your statistic would bounce around if you drew the sample again — and the "so many" is z*, chosen by how often you want to be right. A confidence interval runs that sentence forwards, from the data to a range of plausible parameter values. A significance test runs it backwards: it fixes a value for the parameter, asks how many standard errors away your data landed, and converts that into a p-value. The only real subtleties are bookkeeping, and they are exactly what exams test: the test for one proportion builds its standard error from p₀ because it is working inside the null world, while the interval builds it from p̂ because there is no null world to work in; and for two proportions the test pools the samples while the interval does not. Everything else — plus-four, the successes-and-failures conditions, n = (z*/m)²p*(1−p*) — is there to keep the Normal approximation honest. So: one sentence, two directions, and a small set of conditions that decide whether you are allowed to say it.

Lesson 1 of 14 · Slides 3–7 · statistical estimation

🔭 Is the sky blue?

The lecture opens with a deliberately silly question, because it contains the entire logic of hypothesis testing.

Slides 4 and 5: likely colours for the sky if the statement is true, and unlikely ones either side. The picture the lecturer draws next is a Normal curve running from −4 to +4.

  • 🔭
    You cannot check a claim by looking at one thing. "Is the sky blue?" is answered by deciding which colours the claim makes likely, and which would be a surprise.
  • 0️⃣
    The null hypothesis H₀ is "there is no difference" — both groups equal, or the mean is equal to 0. It is the claim you put on trial.
  • ❓
    Which distribution do we use to test a hypothesis? Not individual sample scores, and not the population scores.
  • ✅
    The answer: the distribution of sample means — the sampling distribution. Everything in this lecture is built on it.
  • 🎯
    And that is why the curve runs from −4 to +4. It is measured in standard errors, so any statistic can be placed on the same picture.

📊 Three distributions students confuse — and only one is used for testing

DistributionWhat it describesUsed for testing?
Individual sample scoresThe values of the people in your one sample.No — it describes your data, not what chance would do.
The populationThe values of everyone, if you could see them all.No — and you do not have it. It is what you are trying to learn about.
The sampling distributionThe values a statistic would take over all possible samples of the same size.Yes. It says how much a sample mean or sample proportion bounces around by chance.
🪝 Memory hook: the sky test. You do not prove "blue" — you list the colours a blue sky is allowed to be, look up, and see whether today's colour is on the list.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "I start by assuming there's nothing going on. Then I work out what my result would probably look like in that world. If what I actually got would be really unusual in that world, I stop believing the assumption."
★ Key insight

The curve from −4 to +4 is not a picture of your data. It is a picture of how much a statistic moves around by chance, and your single result is one point on it. Getting this straight now makes everything after it easy.

🎬 The logic of the null hypothesis 🎬 Sampling distribution vs population

🧩 Comprehension check

Why is the population distribution the wrong one to test against?
Two reasons. You do not have it — if you did, you would not need inference at all. And even if you did, it describes how individuals vary, while your question is about how much a sample statistic varies. Individuals scatter far more than sample means do, which is why the sampling distribution is narrower by a factor of √n.
In your own work: a survey finds 68% of managers feel prepared for a new role. What is the analogue of the "unlikely colours"?
The results a sample of that size would rarely produce if the true figure were, say, 60%. If 68% lies far enough out in that range of chance outcomes, "the true figure is 60%" becomes hard to maintain. The rest of this lecture is how to turn "far enough out" into a number.

Lesson 2 of 14 · Slides 8–10 · the sampling distribution

🎯 How much does a sample mean bounce?

Shape, centre and spread — the three facts that make estimation possible, worked on the lecture's own numbers.

Slide 8. With σ = 20 and n = 16, the standard deviation of x̄ is 20/√16 = 5 — so sample means are four times less variable than individual scores.

  • 📐
    Shape. Since the population is Normal, so is the sampling distribution of x̄.
  • 🎯
    Centre. The mean of the sampling distribution of x̄ is the same as the mean of the population, µ. The sample mean is an unbiased estimator.
  • 📏
    Spread. σx̄ = σ/√n = 20/√16 = 5.
  • 🔢
    That is where the z-score comes from: z = (X − µ)/σ measures how many standard deviations from the mean a value sits.
  • 🧭
    And it makes the interval: with a sample mean of 240.79, µ is within about 10 points either side in roughly 95% of samples — the interval 230.79 to 250.79.

🪝 Memory hook: averaging is noise-cancelling. Individual people are loud and disagree; their average is quiet. Quadruple the crowd and the remaining noise halves.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "Individual scores are all over the place, but averages of 16 people are much steadier. That steadiness has a formula — the population spread divided by the square root of the sample size — and it's what lets me say how close my average probably is to the truth."
★ Key insight

The √n is why precision is expensive. Going from n = 100 to n = 400 halves your margin of error; halving it again costs 1 600. Every "can we just survey a few more people?" conversation is governed by this square root.

🎬 Why σ/√n 🎬 The central limit theorem

🧩 Comprehension check

σ = 20 and n = 100. What is the standard deviation of x̄?
20/√100 = 2. Compare with 5 at n = 16: multiplying the sample by 6.25 has cut the spread by 2.5, which is √6.25. The gain is real but slows down as you go.
The sampling distribution is centred on µ. Why does that matter for estimation?
Because it means x̄ is not systematically too high or too low — it is unbiased. If it were biased, no amount of extra data would fix it: the curve would be narrow but centred in the wrong place, which is exactly what a bad survey design produces.
Where does "10 points either side" come from in the lecture's example?
Roughly two standard errors: 2 × 5 = 10, using the 68-95-99.7 rule. The exact z* for 95% is 1.96, so the precise margin would be 9.8 — the lecture rounds to 10 to keep the idea in view.

Lesson 3 of 14 · Slides 10–12 · the big idea

🧱 Estimate ± margin of error

The shape every confidence interval in this course takes — and what the confidence level is really a statement about.

Slide 12: "All confidence intervals we construct have the following form." The lecture's own words for C: the probability that the interval will contain the true parameter value in repeated sample draws.

  • 🧱
    Every interval has two parts: an interval of the form estimate ± margin of error, and a confidence level C.
  • 🎲
    C is a property of the method, not of your one interval. It is how often intervals built this way capture the true value in repeated samples.
  • 📏
    m = z* σ/√n for a mean. Wider confidence needs a bigger z*, so a 99% interval is always wider than a 95% one.
  • 🎚️
    We usually choose 90% or higher, because we want to be relatively sure of our conclusions. 95% is the most common.
  • 🚫
    What you may not say: "there is a 95% probability that µ is in this interval". Once computed, the interval either contains µ or it does not.

🧪 Demo 2 · Twenty samples, twenty intervals

The picture on slide 11, live. Each line is one sample's confidence interval; the vertical line is the true µ, which in real life you never see. Draw new samples and watch how often an interval misses.

🪝 Memory hook: a confidence interval is a net, not a fish. The confidence level describes how often the net catches; it says nothing about whether this particular throw did.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "I take my estimate and stretch a margin either side of it. The 95% doesn't mean the truth is 95% likely to be in my range — it means if I kept doing this, 95% of the ranges I build would contain the truth."
★ Key insight

Confidence is a claim about the procedure, tested over repeated sampling. That is why the demo shows twenty intervals: the number 95% only becomes visible when you look at many of them, never at one.

🎬 What a confidence interval does not mean 🎬 Margin of error

🧩 Comprehension check

Which is wider: a 90% or a 99% interval from the same data?
The 99% interval, because z* is larger (2.576 against 1.645). More confidence is bought with vagueness: the only way to be surer of catching the parameter, from the same sample, is to cast a wider net.
Your interval for a proportion is (0.375, 0.477) and someone claims p = 0.5. What can you say?
That 0.5 is not among the plausible values at this confidence level, so you have reason to doubt the claim — which is exactly the lecture's conclusion for the beads. A confidence interval doubles as a two-sided test: any value outside it would be rejected at the matching α.
A colleague says "95% of the data lies in this interval". Correct them.
That describes a completely different interval. A confidence interval is about the parameter — the true mean or proportion — and it is typically far narrower than the spread of individual values, because it is built from the standard error, not the standard deviation.

Lesson 4 of 14 · Slides 19–20 · critical values

📏 Where z* comes from

A level C interval keeps area C in the middle of the standard Normal curve. Finding the number that does that is the whole job.

Central area C between −z* and +z*, with (1 − C)/2 left in each tail. Change the level in the demo below and watch z* move.

  • 🎯
    The definition. z* is the critical value for the standard Normal curve with area C between −z* and +z*.
  • ✌️
    The quick version. For 95%, the 68-95-99.7 rule gives a critical value of about 2 — and a table or calculator sharpens it to 1.96.
  • 🔍
    Finding one by hand: for an 80% interval you leave out 20%, or 10% in each tail, and search Table A for the point with area 0.1 to its left — z = −1.28, so z* = 1.28.
  • 🧠
    Learn three by heart: 90% → 1.645, 95% → 1.96, 99% → 2.576. They cover almost every exam question.
  • ↔️
    Higher confidence, larger z*, wider interval. There is no way around that trade — only a bigger n can buy back the width.

🎬 Finding z* in Table A

🧩 Comprehension check

For a 98% interval, what area is in each tail, and roughly what is z*?
2% is left out, so 1% in each tail — you look for the point with area 0.01 to its left, which is z = −2.326, giving z* = 2.326. Sensibly between the 95% and 99% values.
Why does the slide say the critical value is "actually 1.96" rather than 2?
Because the 68-95-99.7 rule is a rounded teaching device: exactly 95% of the standard Normal curve lies between ±1.96, while ±2 captures 95.4%. For coursework use 1.96; the difference matters once samples get large.

Lesson 5 of 14 · Slides 16–17 · 8.1 begins

🪙 The sampling distribution of a proportion

Same three facts as for a mean — shape, centre, spread — but now the spread depends on p itself.

The sampling distribution of p̂ for the p and n you choose in the demo. As n increases it becomes approximately Normal: for large n, p̂ has approximately the N(p, √(p(1−p)/n)) distribution.

  • 🎯
    Centre. The mean of the sampling distribution is p — the sample proportion is unbiased.
  • 📏
    Spread. σp̂ = √(p(1−p)/n). Notice the spread is decided by p and n, with no separate σ to be given.
  • 📐
    Shape. As n increases the sampling distribution becomes approximately Normal.
  • 🎚️
    Spread is largest at p = 0.5 and shrinks towards the extremes — a fact Lesson 9 uses to choose sample sizes.
  • ⚠️
    Near 0 or 1 the Normal shape fails, because the distribution is squashed against a boundary. That is what the successes-and-failures conditions are guarding against.

🪝 Memory hook: p(1−p) is a seesaw — highest in the middle, tipping to nothing at either end. Uncertainty is greatest when the population is genuinely split.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "A sample percentage jumps around from sample to sample, and how much it jumps has a formula: the square root of p times one minus p, over n. Bigger samples, steadier percentages."
★ Key insight

For a proportion, the mean and the spread are tied together — both come from p. That is unlike a mean, where µ and σ are separate, and it is the reason the significance test in Lesson 8 can use p₀ in its standard error while the confidence interval cannot.

🎬 The sampling distribution of p̂

🧩 Comprehension check

p = 0.5 and n = 100. What is the standard deviation of p̂?
√(0.5 × 0.5 / 100) = √0.0025 = 0.05, or five percentage points. So even with a hundred people, a sample percentage routinely lands ten points either side of the truth — worth remembering before reading much into a small survey.
Two surveys, both n = 400: one about something with p ≈ 0.5, one with p ≈ 0.05. Which estimate is more precise?
The rare one. √(0.05 × 0.95/400) = 0.011 against √(0.25/400) = 0.025. Rare events are estimated more precisely in absolute terms — though in relative terms the picture reverses, which is the trap the relative-risk lesson closes on.

Lesson 6 of 14 · Slides 18, 21–22 · the one-sample z interval

🫙 The interval for a single proportion

Same method as for means, with one substitution — and one condition you must state.

Slides 18 and 21. The interval uses the observed p̂ inside the standard error — remember this when you meet the significance test, which does not.

  • 🔁
    Same method as for means: estimate ± z* × standard error. Only the standard error changes.
  • 🧮
    SE of a sample proportion = √(p̂(1−p̂)/n), using the sample value in place of the unknown p.
  • 📋
    State the conditions. An SRS, and successes and failures both at least 10.
  • 🫙
    The beads. 107 red out of 251 → p̂ = 0.426; SE = 0.0312; m = 1.645 × 0.0312 = 0.051; interval (0.375, 0.477).
  • 🎯
    The interpretation, in the lecture's words: we are 90% confident that the interval from 0.375 to 0.477 captures the actual proportion of red beads — and since 0.5 is not in it, we have reason to doubt the instructor's claim. (Carrying the decimals through gives 0.4776, so the calculator below shows 0.478; the slide rounds p̂ to 0.426 first.)

🎬 A one-proportion interval, worked

🧩 Comprehension check

Why does the interval use p̂ inside the square root rather than p?
Because p is unknown — it is the thing being estimated. The interval substitutes the best available estimate, which is why the result is called a standard error rather than a standard deviation, and why the procedure needs a decent sample size to behave.
A sample of 40 has 6 successes. Can you use this interval?
No. There are 6 successes, below the required 10, so the large-sample interval is not valid. This is exactly the situation the plus-four method in the next lesson was invented for.
What would happen to the beads interval at 95% confidence instead of 90%?
z* rises from 1.645 to 1.96, so the margin grows from 0.051 to 0.061 and the interval becomes about (0.365, 0.487). Still excluding 0.5 — but note how close: at 99% (z* = 2.576) the margin is 0.080 and 0.5 would be inside, and the claim would survive.

Lesson 7 of 14 · Slides 24–25 · plus-four

➕ Four imaginary observations

A one-line repair for an interval that quietly over-promises.

Slides 24 and 25. The plus-four estimate is (successes + 2) ÷ (n + 4), and the interval is p̃ ± z*√(p̃(1 − p̃)/(n + 4)).

  • ⚠️
    The large-sample interval is easy to understand because it comes straight from the approximate Normal distribution of p̂ — but its true confidence level is usually below what you asked for.
  • ➕
    The fix is absurdly simple: add four imaginary observations — two successes and two failures.
  • 🧮
    p̃ = (X + 2)/(n + 4), and then the same interval formula with n + 4 underneath.
  • 🎯
    Its effect is to pull the estimate towards 0.5, which widens intervals that were too narrow near the edges.
  • 📉
    It matters most in small samples. With n = 1 500 the correction moves nothing; with n = 20 it changes the answer.

🪝 Memory hook: two in, two out — invite two imaginary successes and two imaginary failures to the party, and the interval starts behaving.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "The normal formula is a bit over-confident with small samples — it gives intervals that are too narrow. The trick is to pretend you saw four extra people, two of each kind, and then do exactly the same calculation."
★ Key insight

Plus-four is not a different theory — it is the same interval applied to a slightly doctored dataset. That is why an exam can ask for either, and why the only thing you must not do is mix them: p̃ goes with n + 4, never with n.

🎬 The plus-four interval

🧩 Comprehension check

X = 3 successes out of 20. Give both intervals at 95%.
Large-sample: p̂ = 0.150, SE = √(0.15 × 0.85/20) = 0.0798, so 0.150 ± 0.156 → (−0.007, 0.306) — and a negative proportion should tell you the method has broken. Plus-four: p̃ = 5/24 = 0.208, SE = √(0.208 × 0.792/24) = 0.0829, so 0.208 ± 0.163 → (0.045, 0.371). Sensible, and inside the possible range.
Why does adding two and two pull the estimate towards 0.5?
Because the added observations are split evenly. If your sample was lopsided, the four neutral cases drag the proportion towards the middle; if your sample was already near 0.5, they change almost nothing.

Lesson 8 of 14 · Slides 26–28 · the z test

🥔 Testing a claim about one proportion

The other direction of travel: fix a value, and ask how strange the data would be if it were true.

Slides 26–28. The z statistic has approximately the standard Normal distribution when H₀ is true, which is why P-values come from the standard Normal distribution.

  • 📜
    The hypotheses. H₀: p = p₀ against a one- or two-sided Ha. For the potatoes, H₀: p = 0.08, Ha: p > 0.08.
  • 🧮
    The statistic. z = (p̂ − p₀) ÷ √(p₀(1−p₀)/n) — the distance from the claim, in standard errors.
  • 🔑
    The standard error uses p₀, not p̂, because the test asks what would happen if H₀ were true.
  • 📉
    The P-value is the probability of a z this large or larger in the direction specified by Ha.
  • ⚖️
    The decision. 0.1251 > α = 0.10, so we fail to reject H₀: not sufficient evidence that more than 8% of the potatoes are blemished. The truckload is used.

🎬 The one-proportion z test 🎬 What a P-value actually is

🧩 Comprehension check

Why is the Normal condition checked with np₀ rather than with the observed successes?
Because the whole test lives inside the null world. If H₀ is true, the expected number of blemished potatoes is 500 × 0.08 = 40 and unblemished 460 — those are the counts whose size decides whether the Normal approximation to the sampling distribution is safe.
The P-value is 0.1251. Say precisely what that number means.
If exactly 8% of the potatoes in the truckload were blemished, then about 12.5% of random samples of 500 would show 9.4% blemished or more. It is a statement about samples in a hypothetical world — never the probability that H₀ is true.
Would this test have been significant at α = 0.10 with n = 2 000 and the same 9.4%?
Yes. The standard error is halved to about 0.0061, so z ≈ 2.31 and the P-value falls to about .010. Same effect, four times the data, opposite decision — which is why "not significant" must always be read alongside the sample size.

Lesson 9 of 14 · Slides 29–31 · sample size

📊 How many people do you need?

Run the margin-of-error formula backwards, and make one safe guess.

Slides 30 and 31. Two ways to choose p*: a guess based on past experience or a pilot study, or the safe p* = 0.5, where the margin of error is largest.

  • 🔄
    The formula. n = (z*/m)² × p*(1−p*), where p* is a guessed value for the sample proportion.
  • 🎯
    Why a guess is needed: the margin of error involves p̂, which you do not have before you collect the data.
  • 🛡️
    The safe guess is p* = 0.5, because p(1−p) is largest there — the margin of error will always be less than or equal to m.
  • ⬆️
    Always round up. 1 067.1 becomes 1 068 respondents, because rounding down would leave the margin above what you promised.
  • 💸
    Halving m quadruples n. The m sits squared in the denominator, so precision gets expensive fast.

📊 The lecture's worked answer (slide 31)

"Determine the sample size needed to estimate p within 0.03 with 95% confidence." The critical value for 95% confidence is z* = 1.96, so n = (1.96/0.03)² × 0.5(1 − 0.5) = 1 067.1 — rounded up to 1 068 respondents to ensure the margin of error is no more than 0.03 at 95% confidence.

🪝 Memory hook: p* = 0.5 is the insurance policy. It costs you a few extra respondents and guarantees you never under-deliver on the margin you promised.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "I decide how precise I want to be, look up z* for my confidence level, and the formula tells me how many people to survey. If I've no idea what the percentage will be, I assume 50% because that's the hardest case."
★ Key insight

Sample size is decided by the margin of error you promise, not by the size of the population. Estimating a percentage in a city of 80 000 and in a country of 80 million takes almost identical n, provided the sample stays a small fraction of the population (the 10% condition) — a fact worth having ready the next time someone argues about survey coverage.

🎬 Choosing a sample size

🧩 Comprehension check

Work the lecture's practice question: 90% confidence, m = 0.04.
z* = 1.645, so n = (1.645/0.04)² × 0.25 = (41.125)² × 0.25 = 422.8 → 423 students. Note the lower confidence level makes this far cheaper than the 1 068 needed for 95% at m = 0.03.
A pilot suggests p ≈ 0.1. How much does using that instead of 0.5 save at 95% confidence and m = 0.03?
p*(1−p*) falls from 0.25 to 0.09, so n falls from 1 068 to (1.96/0.03)² × 0.09 = 384.2 → 385. Roughly two-thirds fewer respondents — the payoff for having any prior information at all.
Why round 1 067.1 up rather than to the nearest whole number?
Because 1 067 respondents would give a margin of error slightly larger than 0.03, breaking the specification. Sample size is always rounded up, regardless of the decimal.

Lesson 10 of 14 · Slides 32–41 · the practice question

🏖️ Spring break, end to end

The lecture's own multi-part question, in the order an exam would ask it. Try each part before opening the answer.

📋 The scenario (slide 32)

When the term "spring break" is mentioned, many of us think of Florida. A survey among college students is performed to find out what proportion of students actually does go to Florida during spring break.

The 90% confidence interval for the Florida survey, and the value 0.50 that the significance test puts on trial. The interval sits entirely above 0.50 — which is the same conclusion the test reaches, from the other direction.

  • 1️⃣
    Plan the survey. 90% confidence with a margin of no more than 4 points needs n = (1.645/0.04)² × 0.25 = 422.8 → 423 students.
  • 2️⃣
    The data. The company surveys 1 500 students; 812 say they go — p̂ = 812/1500 = 0.5413.
  • 3️⃣
    The interval. 0.5413 ± 1.645 √(0.5413 × 0.4587/1500) = (0.520, 0.563).
  • 4️⃣
    The test. H₀: p = 0.50 against Ha: p > 0.50 gives z = (0.5413 − 0.50)/√(0.25/1500) = 3.20.
  • 5️⃣
    The P-value. P = 1 − 0.9993 = 0.0007 — strong evidence that a majority of students do go.

🧩 Work it yourself

Part 1 · What is the minimum number of students that needs to be sampled?
n = (z*/m)² p*(1−p*) with z* = 1.645 (90%), m = 0.04 and the safe guess p* = 0.5:
n = (1.645/0.04)² × 0.25 = (41.125)² × 0.25 = 422.82, rounded up to 423 students.
Part 2 · Calculate a 90% confidence interval from the 1 500 surveyed, of whom 812 said yes.
p̂ = 812/1500 = 0.5413. SE = √(0.5413 × 0.4587/1500) = 0.01287. m = 1.645 × 0.01287 = 0.0212.
Interval: 0.5413 ± 0.0212 = (0.520, 0.563). Conditions: 812 successes and 688 failures, both far above 10.
Part 3 · Is there evidence that the majority of students go? Test H₀: p = 0.50 against Ha: p > 0.50.
z = (p̂ − p₀)/√(p₀(1−p₀)/n) = (0.5413 − 0.50)/√(0.25/1500) = 0.0413/0.01291 = 3.20.
P = 1 − 0.9993 = 0.0007. Far below any conventional α, so we reject H₀: there is strong evidence that more than half of students go to Florida during spring break.
Part 4 · Why does the standard error change between part 2 and part 3?
In the interval there is no claimed value, so the SE uses p̂ = 0.5413 and gives 0.01287. In the test we work inside H₀, so the SE uses p₀ = 0.50 and gives 0.01291. The two are nearly identical here because p̂ is close to 0.50 — but the reasoning behind them is completely different, and when p̂ is far from p₀ the numbers differ noticeably.
Part 5 · The interval and the test agree. Is that a coincidence?
No. A one-sided test at α = 0.05 and a 90% two-sided interval are close relatives: any value outside the interval would be rejected as a null value at the matching level. Here 0.50 lies below the lower limit of 0.520, which is the same news as p = 0.0007.
🪝 Memory hook: plan, collect, estimate, test. Every proportion question in this course is some subset of those four steps, in that order.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "First I work out how many people I need. Then I collect the data and give a range for the true percentage. Then, if someone has a specific claim — like 'it's only half' — I test it and get a p-value."
★ Key insight

The company sampled 1 500 when 423 would have met the specification. That is not waste in the exam's eyes — the extra data shrank the margin from 4 points to about 2 — but it is exactly the trade you will be asked to justify in real life: precision costs, and the curve flattens.

⚖️ Two groups, two proportions

The second half of the lecture. Everything doubles — but the three facts about the sampling distribution stay the same three facts.

Slides 43 and 44. Two independent samples, one difference, and a sampling distribution with the same shape–centre–spread structure as everything in Lesson 2.

  • 👥
    Two situations, same maths. Comparing the proportions with some characteristic in two populations, or comparing two treatments in a completely randomised experiment.
  • 🎯
    Centre. The mean of the sampling distribution is p₁ − p₂ — the difference in sample proportions is unbiased.
  • 📏
    Spread. √(p₁(1−p₁)/n₁ + p₂(1−p₂)/n₂). The two variances add, because each sample brings its own uncertainty.
  • 📐
    Shape. Approximately Normal when the samples are large.
  • 🔗
    Independence is essential. The two samples must be taken independently of each other — this is not for paired or repeated measures.
🪝 Memory hook: variances add, standard errors don't. Square, add, root — in that order, every single time you combine two sources of noise.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "I've got two separate samples and I care about the gap between their percentages. That gap also bounces around, and its bounce is the two bounces combined — squared, added, square-rooted."
★ Key insight

Because the two variances add, a comparison is always noisier than either single estimate. That is why a difference needs more data to establish than a proportion does — and why small pilot comparisons so often come back "not significant".

🎬 The distribution of p̂₁ − p̂₂

🧩 Comprehension check

Why can't you just subtract the two standard errors?
Because uncertainty never cancels. Both samples are noisy, and the noise in the difference is bigger than the noise in either one. Mathematically it is the variances that add: SE = √(SE₁² + SE₂²), which is always larger than either SE alone.
Two groups of 100, p̂₁ = 0.6 and p̂₂ = 0.5. What is the SE of the difference?
√(0.6×0.4/100 + 0.5×0.5/100) = √(0.0024 + 0.0025) = √0.0049 = 0.07. The observed difference of 0.10 is only about 1.4 standard errors — nowhere near significant, which surprises most people looking at "60% versus 50%".

Lesson 12 of 14 · Slides 45–50 · the two-sample interval

🌐 How big is the gap?

The teens-and-adults example, and the plus-four version for when the samples are small.

Slide 49: 73% of 800 teens and 47% of 2 253 adults use social-networking sites. The difference is 0.26 and the 95% interval runs from 0.223 to 0.297 — entirely above zero.

  • 🧮
    The statistic. p̂₁ − p̂₂ is our best guess for p₁ − p₂.
  • 📏
    The standard error. SE = √(p̂₁(1−p̂₁)/n₁ + p̂₂(1−p̂₂)/n₂) — each sample's own proportion, never pooled.
  • 🎯
    The interval. (p̂₁ − p̂₂) ± z* × SE, with z* from the confidence level as always.
  • 📋
    The conditions. Random: two random samples or two groups in a randomised experiment. Normal: the counts of successes and failures in each group — n₁p̂₁, n₁(1−p̂₁), n₂p̂₂, n₂(1−p̂₂) — are all at least 10.
  • ➕
    Plus-four for two samples: add one success and one failure to each sample, then use the usual formula with n₁ + 2 and n₂ + 2.

📊 The lecture's arithmetic (slide 49)

(0.73 − 0.47) ± 1.96 √(0.73 × 0.27/800 + 0.47 × 0.53/2253) = 0.26 ± 0.037 = (0.223, 0.297).

And the interpretation, in the lecturer's words: we are 95% confident that the interval from 0.223 to 0.297 captures the true difference in the proportion of all U.S. teens and adults who use social-networking sites — more teens than adults, by between 22.3 and 29.7 percentage points.

🪝 Memory hook: for an interval, the two groups keep their own money — each contributes its own p̂ to the standard error. Pooling is for the test, and only for the test.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "I subtract the two percentages to get the gap, work out how much that gap wobbles by combining both samples' wobble, and put z* times that either side. If the range doesn't include zero, the two groups really do differ."
★ Key insight

An interval for a difference does two jobs at once: it tests (does it contain zero?) and it sizes (how big is the gap, at worst and at best?). Reporting only "significant" throws away the second, which is usually the half your audience needs.

🎬 The two-proportion interval

🧩 Comprehension check

The interval is (0.223, 0.297). What would it mean if it had been (−0.02, 0.31)?
That zero is a plausible value for the difference, so the data would not establish any gap between teens and adults — even though the point estimate is still 0.145. A wide interval spanning zero usually means small samples, not a small effect.
Check the Normal condition for the teens-and-adults study.
Teens: 0.73 × 800 = 584 successes and 216 failures. Adults: 0.47 × 2 253 = 1 059 successes and 1 194 failures. All four counts are far above 10, so the condition is met — which is what the slide means by "they are all greater than 10".
How would the plus-four version of this interval differ?
Barely at all. You would use (584+1)/802 = 0.729 and (1059+1)/2255 = 0.470, moving the difference by a thousandth. Plus-four earns its keep when samples are small; with 800 and 2 253 it is a rounding error.

Lesson 13 of 14 · Slides 51–55 · the two-sample test

🥣 Pooling, and why only here

The breakfast study — and the single most examinable distinction in Chapter 8.

Slides 51–53. The pooled proportion is the count of successes in both samples combined, divided by the count of individuals in both samples combined.

  • 🤔
    Why the test pools. If H₀: p₁ = p₂ is true, the two parameters are the same — so it makes sense to combine the data to estimate that common value.
  • 🧮
    The pooled proportion. p̂ = (successes in both samples) ÷ (individuals in both samples) = 45/230 = 0.1957.
  • 📐
    The statistic. z = (p̂₁ − p̂₂) ÷ √(p̂(1−p̂)(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)) = 0.0642 ÷ 0.0549 = 1.17.
  • 📉
    The P-value. Two-sided: 2P(z ≥ 1.17) = 2(1 − 0.8790) = 0.2420. Greater than α = 0.05, so we fail to reject H₀.
  • 🗣️
    The conclusion. Not sufficient evidence that the proportions of students who did not eat breakfast differ between the two schools.

⚠️ A wrinkle worth knowing about

The slides state the Normal condition twice with different numbers: at least 10 successes and failures per group for the confidence interval (slide 46), and at least 5 for the significance test (slides 52–53). Both appear in Moore & McCabe; the difference is a textbook convention rather than something you can derive from the formulas. If an exam question asks you to state the condition, quote the one attached to the procedure you are performing — and if you have to choose, the stricter 10 is never wrong.

🪝 Memory hook: the test believes H₀; the interval does not. Believing it means the two groups share one proportion, so you pool. Not believing it means each group keeps its own.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "For the test I pretend both groups have the same true percentage, so I lump all the successes together to estimate it. For the interval I'm not assuming that, so I keep them separate."
★ Key insight

Pooled and unpooled standard errors usually differ by a few percent, so you will often reach the same decision either way. Examiners know this — which is exactly why they ask you to write the formula rather than the verdict.

🎬 The two-proportion z test 🎬 Pooled vs unpooled

🧩 Comprehension check

Compute the pooled proportion for the breakfast study and say what it represents.
(19 + 26)/(80 + 150) = 45/230 = 0.1957. It is the single best estimate of the common proportion of children skipping breakfast if H₀ is true and the two schools are really the same. It is not the average of 0.2375 and 0.1733 — it is weighted by sample size, which is why it sits closer to the larger school's value.
The P-value is 0.2420. Why was it doubled?
Because Ha: p₁ − p₂ ≠ 0 is two-sided — a difference in either direction counts as evidence. So the tail probability P(z ≥ 1.17) = 0.1210 is counted at both ends: 2 × 0.1210 = 0.2420.
School 1 had 23.8% skipping breakfast against School 2's 17.3%. Does "not significant" mean the schools are the same?
No. With 80 and 150 students the study simply cannot resolve a gap of 6.4 points — the standard error is about 5.5 points (5.7 if you do not pool). The honest conclusion is that this study does not establish a difference, and the confidence interval for the difference (roughly −0.05 to 0.18) shows exactly how much remains unknown.

➗ Ratios, and the last practice question

The third way to compare two proportions — and the reason a doubled risk can still be nothing to worry about.

Slides 56–58, plus the lecture's cartoon about the difference between absolute and relative risk, which is the point of the red panel.

  • ➗
    Relative risk is the ratio of two proportions rather than their difference.
  • 1️⃣
    RR = 1 means the two proportions are equal — the null value for a ratio is one, not zero.
  • 👶
    The study. Women with a first birth at 30 or later: 683 of 3 220 "really happy" (21.2%). Under 30: 1 498 of 10 245 (14.6%). RR = 0.212/0.146 = 1.45.
  • 🗣️
    Say it carefully: women with a late first child have 1.45 times the "risk" of being really happy — an association in an observational study, not an instruction about when to have children.
  • 💻
    Confidence intervals for RR are more complicated (use software) but rest on the same principles.

🧩 The final practice question, worked (slides 59–68)

A manufacturer has two insecticide formulations, using compounds A and B. Insects were released into an enclosure, which was then sprayed. Independent studies were run for each formulation.

Compound ACompound B
Number of insects released200180
Number of insects killed135108
a) Estimate the difference between the true proportions killed, pA − pB.
135/200 = 0.675 and 108/180 = 0.600, so the estimated difference is 0.075.
b) What is the standard error of the estimated difference?
√(0.675 × 0.325/200 + 0.600 × 0.400/180) = √(0.0010969 + 0.0013333) = √0.0024302 = 0.0493. Note this is the unpooled SE — it is the one that belongs with a confidence interval or a descriptive statement about the gap.
c) Test H₀: pA − pB = 0 against Ha: pA − pB > 0. What is the test statistic?
The test pools: p̂ = (135 + 108)/(200 + 180) = 243/380 = 0.639.
z = 0.075 ÷ √(0.639 × 0.361 × (1/200 + 1/180)) = 0.075 ÷ 0.0493 = 1.52. (The pooled and unpooled standard errors agree to three decimals here, which is why the lecture can reuse the number.)
d) What is the P-value, and what should the company conclude?
One-sided: P = 1 − 0.9357 = 0.064. Above α = 0.05, so the result is not significant — there is not enough evidence that compound A kills a greater proportion of insects than compound B. With 200 and 180 insects, a 7.5-point gap is still within chance.
🪝 Memory hook: difference has a null of 0; ratio has a null of 1. If you remember nothing else about relative risk, remember which number means "no effect".
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "You can compare two percentages by subtracting them or by dividing them. Dividing gives a relative risk — 1.45 times as likely. It sounds bigger than it is unless you also say what the two percentages actually were."
★ Key insight

Difference and ratio are both true and can tell opposite stories: a drug that cuts a risk from 2 in 100 000 to 1 in 100 000 halves the relative risk and changes the absolute risk by 0.001%. The lecture's cartoon makes the point exactly — twice as rich when you have two cents is still broke.

🎬 Relative vs absolute risk 🎬 Interpreting a relative risk

🧩 Comprehension check

A report says a benefit "doubles the chance of promotion". What do you ask?
For the two absolute percentages and the sample sizes. Doubling from 1% to 2% and from 20% to 40% are both "doubling", and only one of them would change a policy. Then ask whether people were assigned the benefit or chose it — the design question applies here just as hard.
The happiness study is observational. What follows?
That 1.45 is an association, not an effect. Women who have a first child after 30 differ from those who have one earlier in education, income, partnership and health — any of which could drive the happiness difference. The number is real; the causal reading is not supplied by it.
When would you report a difference rather than a relative risk?
Whenever the audience has to act on the size of the effect — how many extra people are affected. Differences answer "how many"; ratios answer "how many times". Good practice is to give both, which is exactly what the happiness slide does: 1.45 times, and 21.2% against 14.6%.

🧵 Where this leaves you

★ The thesis of the whole lecture

Every procedure in Chapter 8 is the same sentence with different nouns: your estimate, plus or minus so many standard errors. The standard error is the spread of the sampling distribution — how much your statistic would bounce if you drew the sample again — and the "so many" is z*, chosen by how often you want to be right. A confidence interval runs that sentence forwards, from data to a range of plausible values. A significance test runs it backwards: fix a value, count the standard errors, convert to a P-value. The subtleties are bookkeeping, and they are what the exam tests: the one-sample test builds its standard error from p₀ because it works inside the null world, while the interval builds it from p̂; and for two proportions the test pools and the interval does not. Everything else — plus-four, the successes-and-failures conditions, n = (z*/m)²p*(1−p*) — exists to keep the Normal approximation honest. So: one sentence, two directions, and a small set of conditions that decide whether you are allowed to say it.
